General Relativity

You have probably heard that moving clocks run slow and moving rulers shrink. This article shows what that would actually look like, using interactive simulations.

by Pavel Potoček

Meet the white spaceship 🚀—your guide through the world of general relativity. You can control it with your mouse or by touch. For now, it is flying through a classical, flat spacetime.

The ship is absurdly fast—it accelerates at millions of g's, and in just two seconds, it can reach the speed of light, c. Because relativity is not taken into account yet, the speed of light is not a limit.

These simulations take place in three-dimensional spacetime—two spatial dimensions and one time dimension. This lets us see an overview of the universe from the outside.

As we know, in relativity, the speed of light cannot be exceeded. As we get closer to it, lengths contract, and acceleration, as seen by a stationary observer, slows down.

The amount of length contraction, as well as time dilation and other effects, is given by the Lorentz factor, γ.

The figure above shows what the ship would see if it could magically see the whole universe at once—its plane of simultaneity. In reality, it can only see the light that is arriving at it right now—its past light cone. This introduces a new effect to the view—the relativistic Doppler effect:

  • Light ahead is blueshifted and more intense, and light behind is redshifted and less intense.
  • The view of the world around the ship crowds into a cone in front of it, and thins out behind it.

Speeds this close to the speed of light are not just theoretical—high-energy particles in particle accelerators and in cosmic rays can reach well beyond 99.999% of the speed of light.

Now, it's time for the twin paradox. If the spaceship leaves the Earth and returns, it will have aged less than the stationary observer on Earth.

I added clocks to the simulation. Both clocks are viewed from the spaceship perspective - the clock on Earth shows what an observer from the spaceship would see from the distance.

Another interesting effect can be seen in the simulation below. As the spaceship accelerates away from the Earth, the Earth appears to initially move closer to it. And when the spaceship accelerates back, the Earth appears to initially move away from it.

This is what Rocky was confused about in Project Hail Mary, because he didn't know about special relativity.

To keep things simple, the simulated universe is flat in one more sense: space has only two dimensions. The ship moves in a plane, and so does light. We look down on that plane with the ship in the center. Each point around it shows where the light arriving at the ship right now came from. Its direction is the direction the light came from, and its distance from the ship is how far the light traveled, which is also how long ago it left. A grid is painted on space so you can see how it moves and distorts.

Moving through flat space

Start without gravity, in the flat spacetime of special relativity. The speed of light is the same for everyone, and nothing goes faster.

Lengths contract. An object moving past you at speed v is shorter along its direction of motion, by the Lorentz factor γ=11−v2/c2. At 90% of the speed of light, γ≈2.3. This is not an optical illusion. Measuring a length means marking both ends at the same moment, and observers moving relative to each other disagree on which moments are simultaneous. Seen from a moving ship, the grid really is shorter along the direction of motion.

But that's not what you see. Your eyes, or your camera, catch light that left different parts of an object at different times. The grid ahead of you is rushing toward you, so light from its far parts set out earlier, when they were even farther away. That stretches the view ahead. Behind you it's the other way around, and the view is squeezed. Together with the contraction, things straight ahead look longer by a factor 1+β1−β, where β=v/c, and things straight behind look shorter by the same factor. At 90% of light speed, that's a factor of about 4.4 either way.

Light piles up ahead. Light from the sides appears to come from farther ahead, like rain hitting the windshield of a moving car. This is called aberration. The view of the world around you crowds into a cone in front of you, and behind you it thins out. The light ahead is also Doppler-shifted to higher energies: you run into the waves faster. Both effects make the view ahead much brighter and bluer, and the view behind dimmer and redder. This is called relativistic beaming. In the simulation, the grid glows like a warm white light source. Its color and brightness follow the shift: bluish ahead, orange behind.

To see what relativity changes, start with a world without it. In figure 1, light travels infinitely fast, and the ship sees everything around it as it is right now. Figure 2 shows the same flight in flat spacetime.

Black hole

Two months after Einstein published general relativity in 1915, Karl Schwarzschild found the first exact solution of its equations: the spacetime around a single, non-rotating mass. If that mass is squeezed within the Schwarzschild radius rs=2GM/c2, it becomes a black hole. The sphere at rs is its event horizon, and nothing that crosses it can ever get out. In the figure below, the ship falls in from rest far away. Nothing special happens locally as it crosses the horizon, yet from there it reaches the singularity at the center in a finite time of its own. The simulation uses Gullstrand–Painlevé coordinates. In them, space can be pictured as flowing into the black hole like a river, faster and faster, reaching the speed of light at the horizon, and the falling ship is carried along with it.
