General Relativity

You have probably heard that moving clocks run slow and moving rulers shrink. This article shows what that would actually look like, using interactive simulations.

by Pavel Potoček

Meet the white spaceship 🚀 - your guide through the world of general relativity. You can control it with your mouse or touch. For now, it is flying through a classical, flat spacetime.

The ship accelerates absurdly fast - in just two seconds, it can reach the speed of light, c. Because relativity is not taken into account yet, speed of light is not a limit.

These simulations take place in three-dimensional space-time - two spatial dimensions and one temporal dimension. This allows us to see the overview of the universe from the outside.

As we know, in relativity, speed of light can not be exceeded. As we get closer to it, lengths contracts, and acceleration, as seen from the stationary observer, slows down.

The amount of length contraction is given by the Lorentz factor, γ.

To keep things simple, the simulated universe is flat in one more sense: space has only two dimensions. The ship moves in a plane, and so does light. We look down on that plane with the ship in the centre. Each point around it shows where the light arriving at the ship right now came from. Its direction is the direction the light came from, and its distance from the ship is how far the light travelled, which is also how long ago it left. A grid is painted on space so you can see how it moves and distorts.

Moving through flat space

Start without gravity, in the flat spacetime of special relativity. The speed of light is the same for everyone, and nothing goes faster.

Lengths contract. An object moving past you at speed v is shorter along its direction of motion, by the Lorentz factor γ=11−v2/c2. At 90% of the speed of light, γ≈2.3. This is not an optical illusion. Measuring a length means marking both ends at the same moment, and observers moving relative to each other disagree on which moments are simultaneous. Seen from a moving ship, the grid really is shorter along the direction of motion.

But that's not what you see. Your eyes, or your camera, catch light that left different parts of an object at different times. The grid ahead of you is rushing towards you, so light from its far parts set out earlier, when they were even farther away. That stretches the view ahead. Behind you it's the other way round, and the view is squeezed. Together with the contraction, things straight ahead look longer by a factor 1+β1−β, where β=v/c, and things straight behind look shorter by the same factor. At 90% of light speed, that's a factor of about 4.4 either way.

Light piles up ahead. Light from the sides appears to come from further ahead, like rain hitting the windscreen of a moving car. This is called aberration. The view of the world around you crowds into a cone in front of you, and behind you it thins out. The light ahead is also Doppler-shifted to higher energies: you run into the waves faster. Both effects make the view ahead much brighter and bluer, and the view behind dimmer and redder. This is called relativistic beaming. In the simulation, the grid glows like a warm white light source. Its colour and brightness follow the shift: bluish ahead, orange behind.

Black hole

Two months after Einstein published general relativity in 1915, Karl Schwarzschild found the first exact solution of its equations: the spacetime around a single, non-rotating mass. If that mass is squeezed within the Schwarzschild radius rs=2GM/c2, it becomes a black hole. The sphere at rs is its event horizon, and nothing that crosses it can ever get out. In the figure below, the ship falls in from rest far away. Nothing special happens locally as it crosses the horizon, yet from there it reaches the singularity at the centre in a finite time of its own. The simulation uses Gullstrand–Painlevé coordinates. In them, space can be pictured as flowing into the black hole like a river, faster and faster, reaching the speed of light at the horizon, and the falling ship is carried along with it.
